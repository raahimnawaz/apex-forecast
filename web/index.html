Race forecast

Finishing-order probabilities for the next race, from the Layer 1 strength posterior pushed one round forward along each constructor's development trend and conditioned on the actual qualifying grid. Sampled 400 times per posterior draw, so these are joint outcomes rather than independent guesses. Conditional on finishing — retirements are a reliability process and are modelled separately, so treat these as pace-driven probabilities among cars that see the flag.

    Is the forecast any good?

    Walk-forward scoring: for each evaluation round the model is refitted on everything before it and asked to predict a race it has never seen. Naive baselines are given the same treatment — each is a fitted Plackett-Luce distribution rather than a point prediction, so "starting position predicts the finish" competes on equal terms. Lower RPS and log-loss are better. These numbers are published exactly as measured.

        Driver skill versus car

        The hard question in Formula 1: how much of a result is the driver? A Bayesian rank-ordered logit fitted across 2025 and 2026 separates the two, with driver skill carried across the regulation change and constructor performance deliberately severed at it. Units are log-odds — a gap of 1 means roughly e times the odds of finishing ahead. The intervals are wide on purpose: within a fixed team-mate pairing, skill and car can trade off, and only drivers changing teams pin the split down.

            What this model does and does not know

            • Ten races of in-regulation data. 2026 reset the power units, the aerodynamics and the car dimensions, so pre-2026 pace does not transfer. The intervals here are wide because the evidence is genuinely thin, and they should not be narrowed by borrowing from 2025.
            • The driver/car split is weakly identified. Each team fields exactly two cars, so adding a constant to both team-mates and subtracting it from their car leaves the likelihood unchanged. What breaks that tie is drivers moving between teams — 19 drivers appear in both seasons — plus hierarchical shrinkage. Read the split as indicative, and the intervals as the real answer.
            • Retirements are excluded from the strength model. A DNF is a reliability event, not a pace event; keeping retired cars in the ranking would let a gearbox failure look like a slow car. That means the forecast is conditional on finishing, and a full race prediction needs the reliability and safety-car layers that are not built yet.
            • Only the top ten positions are modelled as an ordering. Being classified 15th usually means damage, a penalty or a bad stop — noise about pace. Modelling the full order put a driver with six wins seventh in the forecast; truncating to the points positions fixed it. Positions outside the top ten still inform every comparison, they are just not read as a precise ranking.
            • Driver skill is constant within 2026. With ten races and two cars per team, letting both skill and car drift race-to-race is not identified. The flexibility went to the car, which is where the in-season signal actually is.
            • Cadillac has no history at all. Its estimates rest entirely on ten races as a debut team, and its interval is the widest on the grid for good reason.
            • Known bias: backmarkers spend more time in traffic and lift for blue flags. The dirty-air term absorbs some of that, but not all, so the tail of the field is likely flattered less than it deserves.
            • Compound ordering is not resolved: medium and soft degradation differ by less than their spread across races, so this data cannot rank them.
            • Incident races are read as pace, and it distorts the forecast. Retirements are excluded, but a race where a driver is hit and still finishes 15th is kept — and looks identical to a slow car. Mercedes qualifies at the front almost every weekend yet has the grid's worst average grid-to-finish delta, entirely because of three incident races. Because the grid term already accounts for "they should win", that residual lands on the car, which is why the forecast is harsher on Mercedes than their pace deserves. A contamination component in the likelihood, and the reliability layer, are what fix this.
            • Two independent checks agree. Layer 0 measures pace from lap times; Layer 1 infers strength from finishing order. They share no data representation, likelihood or fitting method, and their constructor rankings correlate at —. Sampler diagnostics: —.
            • Why team colours never encode a value here. Run through a colour-vision validator, the 2026 constructor palette fails outright on the dark surface: Haas silver and Mercedes teal are ΔE 0.6 apart under deuteranopia — effectively the same colour — and Alpine against Red Bull is ΔE 9.2 apart under normal vision, below the legibility floor. So team colour is a chip beside a text label and nothing more; every quantitative scale on this page uses a validated ramp instead.
